## 1. Introduction

Encrypted-traffic classifiers are getting larger and their deployment targets are not. The models that set the state of the art on public benchmarks are multimodal CNNs, transformers and pre-trained "traffic foundation models" with millions to hundreds of millions of parameters [mm_cesnet_v2; ET-BERT; YaTC; netFound], while the places they must run — line-rate probes, edge gateways, customer-premises equipment — offer a fraction of a CPU core per flow. Knowledge distillation is the standard answer to this mismatch, and a growing body of work applies it to traffic classification [NetClus 2508.02282; MERLOT 2411.13004; ResAware 2606.17462; CipherSight 2608.13905; TNSM 2026 federated KD]. Almost without exception, that work reports one thing about the student: its accuracy on a held-out split of the same dataset.

Accuracy is not what makes a traffic classifier deployable. Traffic changes: the year-long CESNET-TLS-Year22 capture shows classifiers losing tens of points of macro-F1 within months of training [Luxemburk 2024; 2506.08655]. Traffic contains things the classifier has never seen, and a model that confidently assigns a new service to an old label is worse than one that abstains [2202.11984]. And traffic datasets are full of shortcuts — features that predict the label in the capture but not in the world — which a model may learn instead of the intended signal [2601.10180]. A large model that handles these three problems well is valuable precisely because of that handling. Whether a small model distilled from it inherits the handling, rather than merely the accuracy, is the question this paper asks, and it has not been asked before in this domain.

The question has a sharper form than "does the student inherit?". Soft targets are a regulariser [Yuan 2020; 2510.12615], and much of what distillation appears to transfer could be produced by any smoothing of the labels, with no teacher involved. To claim that a student has inherited something *from its teacher*, one needs a control in which the teacher changes and nothing else does. We build that control: two teachers of equal accuracy but different architecture, each distilled into the same student, and a test of whether each student's per-flow unknown-scores follow its own teacher more than the other — measured against a directly trained student, because the two teachers are not equally "central". We then ask the same question about the properties operators care about: unknown-traffic detection, calibration, and reliance on shortcut features, and we ask it over a year, because a property inherited at deployment time may not be inherited three months later.

We pre-registered the design, froze it after a pilot and a validation-only design phase, registered it publicly, and only then computed results on 18 test windows spanning 35 weeks (OSF osf.io/rts6n). Ten hypotheses were tested with Holm correction; two were supported and two were reversed.

**Contributions.**
1. **A teacher-swap protocol for measuring inheritance.** We show that a distilled student's per-flow unknown-score pattern shifts measurably toward the specific teacher it was distilled from, relative to a directly trained student, and that this shift exists only at a conventional distillation temperature: the accuracy-optimal temperature transmits nothing teacher-specific.
2. **A negative result on what inheritance buys.** Against the cheapest control — the same student trained directly and temperature-scaled — distillation improves no unknown-detection outcome we measured, and against label smoothing it trades detection for calibration. Inheriting the teacher's score pattern includes inheriting its weakness on near unknowns.
3. **Drift in the wrong direction for the usual worry.** Over 35 weeks, the 11.3M-parameter teacher ensemble loses unknown-detection ability faster than the 101k student; after about three months the student is the better detector.
4. **Shortcuts are a capacity problem, not a distillation problem.** Small students rely on a planted shortcut about twice as much as the teacher regardless of how they are trained, and distillation does not increase that reliance — but a shortcut-reliant teacher does pass over-confidence to a student that never sees the feature.
5. **A released, pre-registered benchmark** on public data: frozen service split, per-flow scores for every model and window, analysis code with a cluster bootstrap that respects the day × service dependence structure of traffic data.

## 2. Related work

**Distillation in traffic classification.** Distillation appears in the traffic literature as a compression tool [NetClus 2508.02282; Pruned Traffic Trees 2608.21874; Future Internet 18(4):197], a cross-environment transfer mechanism [ResAware 2606.17462; CipherSight 2608.13905], a self-supervised pre-training objective [2603.29537], and a component of federated training [TNSM 2026]. ResAware is the closest in spirit: it reports that a website-fingerprinting student inherits some of its teacher's calibration and open-world behaviour, but on a single task, without a teacher-swap control and without a time axis. NetClus distils a traffic transformer and holds out one malware class, on random splits of older datasets. None of these works evaluates the student's unknown-traffic detection against alternatives, its calibration under drift, or its shortcut reliance, and none tests whether what the student learned came from the teacher rather than from soft labels in general.

**What distillation transfers.** Outside networking, the assumption that students inherit their teachers' properties has been examined directly. Stanton et al. [2106.05945] showed that students often fail to match their teachers' outputs even when they match their accuracy; Ojha et al. [2205.16004] and Lukasik et al. [Teacher's Pet] showed that invariances and biases do transfer; Mason-Williams et al. [2510.12615] found across 22 configurations that functional transfer is weaker and more asymmetric than assumed and reframed distillation as a data-dependent regulariser, echoing the label-smoothing equivalence of Yuan et al. [CVPR 2020]. Most relevant to our temperature result, Beyond Dark Knowledge [2606.12171] reports that calibration propagates from teacher to student independently of accuracy and that the temperature governs an accuracy–calibration trade-off; Trust the Uncertain Teacher [2602.12687] and The Role of Teacher Calibration [2508.20224] design or study teachers for that purpose. A recent position paper argues that distillation should be judged on the teacher capabilities it preserves rather than the task score it retains [2604.25110]; our study is evidence of the kind it calls for. None of this work uses teacher identity as a control, evaluates open-set detection as the inherited property, or measures anything over time.

**Whose student is it?** The question of whether a student's behaviour is specific to its teacher has been studied as forensics: tracing an LLM student to its teacher through lexical fingerprints [2502.06659], detecting distillation from a given open-weights model [2510.02302], and watermarking against it [2602.03812; 2608.08139]. We borrow the question and change its purpose: teacher identity is here a control for whether a behavioural property — the ranking of flows by how unknown they look — was inherited, and the directly trained student supplies the baseline that forensic work does not need.

**Open-set and drift-aware traffic classification.** Rejecting unknown services on CESNET data was studied by the dataset authors [2202.11984], and open-world evaluation with leave-one-service-out folds appears in recent darknet work [2608.04167], without distillation. Drift is documented at week scale in the Year22 dataset itself [Luxemburk 2024] and is the subject of a growing critique of evaluation practice: redundancy and leakage in benchmarks [2506.08655], label provenance [2609.02140], distribution-shift robustness [2605.17575; 2608.22683]. Our time-based splits, held-out unknown services, duplicate accounting and pre-registration follow the recommendations of that critique.

**Shortcuts and compression.** Shortcut learning in encrypted-traffic classification was recently mapped across 19 datasets [2601.10180], and spurious-correlation mitigation has entered the traffic literature [2608.30745]. That shortcuts pass through distillation is assumed by a wave of mitigation methods in vision and language [2608.11789; 2608.03632; 2606.05025], and synthetic injected bias features with flip tests have been used to study it in medical imaging [2511.17421]. That compressed models bear a disproportionate share of a model family's brittleness is established for pruning and quantisation [Hooker 2010.03058; 2205.10828; 2110.08419]. We measure rather than mitigate, on traffic, with the condition those works omit: a student that never sees the shortcut, influenced only through soft targets.

**Uncertainty-preserving distillation.** Ensemble distribution distillation [Malinin 2020] and its descendants [2305.10384; 2203.08295; 2503.11339; 2511.13766] report students that match or exceed teacher ensembles at uncertainty estimation; the proxy-Dirichlet formulation we use is due to Ryabinin et al. [2021]. Our negative result for this family is discussed in §Discussion as regime-specific.

**Label smoothing and unknown detection.** Several studies report that label smoothing harms out-of-distribution detection [2007.03212; 2102.05131; 2410.06134; 2403.14715]. Our label-smoothed student is among the better unknown detectors in the grid; §Discussion addresses the difference.
